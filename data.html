Unemployment Benchmarks Breakeven Payrolls Labor Force Trends

What Is a Benchmark Rate of Unemployment?

Policymakers monitor the unemployment rate closely, but the headline number alone does not reveal whether the labor market is running hot or cold. That assessment requires a benchmark.

Two complementary benchmarks emerge depending on the time horizon of interest. The longer-run benchmark reflects the unemployment rate expected to prevail after all cyclical shocks have dissipated. It moves slowly, driven by demographics and structural features of the labor market such as the age composition of the workforce and job-matching efficiency. The stable-price benchmark rate answers a different question: at what level of unemployment would inflation neither accelerate nor decelerate, given current economic conditions? This rate can shift quickly in response to large shocks.

These two benchmarks typically coincide, as they did at roughly 4 percent in late 2019. They can diverge sharply, however, when large disturbances alter the short-run relationship between unemployment and inflation without changing the economy's structural underpinnings.

Technical Details

The estimate comes from a Phillips curve relationship embedded in a state-space model. The observation equation links changes in inflation to the unemployment gap, defined as the difference between the actual unemployment rate and the unobserved natural rate. The natural rate itself follows a random walk, allowing it to drift over time in response to shocks.

We estimate the model's parameters by maximum likelihood using data from 1985:Q1 through 2019:Q4. This sample excludes the pandemic period, so that the extreme observations of 2020 do not distort parameter estimates. The Kalman filter then tracks the unobserved natural rate forward through the most recent quarter of available data.

One parameter, the standard deviation of innovations to the natural rate's random walk, is calibrated rather than estimated. This choice addresses a well-known identification problem: when the natural rate is very smooth, maximum likelihood tends to push its volatility to zero, effectively making the estimate a constant.

Canada. The Canadian stable-price rate uses the same state-space model, estimated over 2000:Q1 through 2019:Q4. The sample begins in 2000 because the exchange-rate control enters as a year-over-year change of the Bank of Canada's effective exchange rate index, which is first published in 1999. The Canadian long-run rate is built from a separate demographic decomposition: the unemployment rate of each age, sex, and education group is weighted by that group's share of the labor force, so the benchmark moves with the changing composition of the workforce. the long-run line shown for Canada is this model estimate.

The model takes three inputs, all expressed as quarterly averages of monthly data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) and the Federal Reserve Economic Data (FRED) system. The first is the civilian unemployment rate. The second is core personal consumption expenditures (PCE) inflation, measured as the year-over-year percent change in the price index excluding food and energy. The third is the broad trade-weighted U.S. dollar index, also expressed as a year-over-year percent change.

The preferred specification uses a counterfactual unemployment rate that adjusts for the surge in temporary layoffs beginning in 2020. This adjustment subtracts excess temporary layoffs, relative to their 2019:Q4 share of total unemployment, from the unemployed stock before computing the unemployment rate. Without this correction, the model interprets the pandemic spike in unemployment as a large increase in the natural rate, which is inconsistent with the transitory nature of the shock.

The estimation sample runs from 1985:Q1 through 2019:Q4. The Kalman filter extends the estimate through the latest available quarter, currently 2026:Q1.

Canada. The Canadian estimates draw on data from Statistics Canada and the Bank of Canada. The stable-price model uses the unemployment rate from the Labour Force Survey, the Bank of Canada's preferred core inflation measure (CPI-trim), and the Canadian effective exchange rate index. The same temporary-layoff adjustment is applied during the pandemic, drawing on the surge in layoffs reported in the Labour Force Survey. The long-run model uses labor force shares and unemployment rates by age, sex, and educational attainment, available monthly back to 1990 and seasonally adjusted with the Census Bureau's X-13 procedure before averaging to quarterly frequency.

Breakeven Payroll Growth

How many jobs does the economy need to create each month to keep the unemployment rate from rising? The answer depends almost entirely on how fast the labor force is growing. If the working-age population is expanding rapidly, the economy needs proportionally more job creation just to absorb new entrants and hold unemployment steady.

This "breakeven" pace of payroll growth can be expressed simply: multiply trend labor force growth by the share of the labor force that is employed. When actual job creation exceeds breakeven, unemployment tends to fall; when it falls short, unemployment tends to rise.

Two complementary horizons are informative. The long-run breakeven captures the slow-moving demographic forces that determine how many workers the economy must absorb over decades. The short-run breakeven captures medium-frequency variation in labor force growth driven by factors such as immigration flows, shifts in labor force participation, and changes in population growth that operate at business-cycle and slightly longer frequencies.

The same framework applies to Canada. Use the toggle above the chart to switch between the two countries. Because Canada's labor force is roughly one-seventh the size of the U.S. labor force, its breakeven pace is correspondingly smaller in absolute terms; comparing actual job creation to the breakeven line within each country is more informative than comparing the levels across countries. For Canada, the actual pace of hiring is measured by establishment-survey employment (the Survey of Employment, Payrolls and Hours), the closest analogue to U.S. nonfarm payrolls.

Reference: Petrosky-Nadeau and Stewart, "Breakeven Employment Growth" FRBSF Economic Letter 2024-18, 2024.

Technical Details

The breakeven formula is: dNbe = (1 − ū) × dLFtrend, where ū is a reference unemployment rate and dLFtrend is the monthly change in the trend labor force level. For the United States, ū is set to 4.4 percent (the CBO's current longer-run natural rate); for Canada, ū is set to 6.0 percent. Empirically relevant changes in ū have a negligible effect on the estimates.

Trend labor force is extracted using a Christiano-Fitzgerald (1999) asymmetric band-pass filter applied to the labor force level — CLF16OV for the United States, the Labour Force Survey labour force for Canada. The filter removes cyclical fluctuations while preserving the underlying growth trend. The methodology is identical across countries; only the underlying data and reference rate differ.

Two filter configurations capture different horizons. The long-run filter passes periods between 2 and 480 months (40 years), extracting the demographic trend from the full available sample (the United States from January 1948, Canada from January 1976). The short-run filter passes periods between 2 and 72 months (6 years), capturing medium-frequency movements. Because the pandemic created a discontinuity in the labor force series in both countries, the short-run filter is applied separately to a pre-COVID sub-period (through February 2020) and a post-COVID sub-period (January 2021 through the latest available month).

Both filters use local drift removal: the long-run filter subtracts a linear trend estimated from the trailing 20-year window, while the short-run filter uses a 3-year trailing window. This approach adapts to the secular deceleration in labor force growth without imposing a full-sample average that would overstate current trend growth.

Band-pass filter endpoint estimates are less precise for the most recent approximately 24 months due to the symmetric nature of the ideal filter being approximated. Interpret the latest long-run estimates with appropriate caution.

For the United States, all data are from the Bureau of Labor Statistics via FRED (Federal Reserve Economic Data). The civilian labor force level (CLF16OV, thousands, seasonally adjusted, monthly) is used to extract trend growth. Total nonfarm payrolls (PAYEMS, thousands, seasonally adjusted, monthly) provide the actual pace of job creation shown as bars in the chart. The reference unemployment rate is 4.4 percent (CBO's current longer-run natural rate).

For Canada, data are from Statistics Canada. Trend growth is extracted from the labour force level (Labour Force Survey, table 14-10-0287, thousands, seasonally adjusted, monthly, beginning January 1976). The actual pace of job creation shown as bars is employment from the Survey of Employment, Payrolls and Hours (SEPH, table 14-10-0223, seasonally adjusted, monthly, beginning January 2001), the establishment-survey analogue of U.S. nonfarm payrolls. The reference unemployment rate is set to 6.0 percent, consistent with the author's own natural-rate estimates.

Estimates are updated following each monthly labor market release. The chart displays data from January 2022 through the latest available month.

Labor Force Trends and Projections

How fast is the labor force really growing, once the swings of the business cycle are stripped away? The answer shapes nearly every question about the labor market, from how many jobs the economy needs each month to how much the workforce will expand over the coming decade. But the raw count of workers is noisy, and participation rates move with the cycle. Isolating the underlying trend requires looking through that variation.

The trend shown below is built from a demographic decomposition of the population. The civilian population is split into narrow cells by sex and age; the participation rate of each cell is smoothed to remove cyclical and seasonal variation, and the cells are recombined using their population shares. This captures two distinct forces: changes in how much each group participates, and the shifting age composition of the population.

The same framework projects the labor force forward. Holding each group's trend participation rate fixed at its latest value, the projection lets official Census population projections drive the changing demographic mix. It is a demographic-composition baseline that answers what would happen to participation and the labor force if the only moving part were the age composition of the population. The dashed line extends this baseline to 2035.

The two charts show the labor force level, in millions of workers, and the participation rate, in percent. In each, the smoothed demographic trend runs through the historical period and joins continuously to the projection.

Reference: Petrosky-Nadeau, "Labor Market Trends and Projections" (methodology note, 2026).

Technical Details

The population is partitioned into cells defined by sex (two groups) and age (16–24, 25–34, 35–44, 45–54, and 55+). For each cell, the monthly participation rate is computed from Current Population Survey microdata back to January 1976. Each cell's series is seasonally adjusted with the Census Bureau's X-13ARIMA-SEATS procedure, the March–November 2020 pandemic months are treated as a transitory disruption and interpolated, a centered three-month moving average removes residual noise, and a Christiano-Fitzgerald band-pass filter extracts the low-frequency trend.

Aggregate trends are weighted averages of the filtered cell series, using each group's smoothed population share as the weight. This decomposition separates changes in group-specific participation behavior from changes in the demographic composition of the population.

The projection holds each cell's trend participation rate fixed at its most recent value and applies Census Bureau population projections to evolve the demographic shares forward to 2035. The aggregate participation rate and labor force level then move only with the changing age and sex composition of the population.

Trend construction uses Current Population Survey microdata from the Bureau of Labor Statistics, January 1976 through the latest available month, with BLS composite weights. The actual labor force level shown in the level chart is the official civilian labor force (FRED series CLF16OV, seasonally adjusted, thousands), which extends through the most recent monthly release.

Population projections that drive the forward path are from the U.S. Census Bureau.

Estimates are updated following each monthly labor market release and after population-projection revisions.
